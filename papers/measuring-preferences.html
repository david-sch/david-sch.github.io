Measuring Preferences for Local Governments

David Schönholzer

Abstract

Local governments provide many essential public goods and services, such as education, policing, and public infrastructure. Using a sample of 1.5 million homes near thousands of local government boundaries, we estimate the value of accessing these governments, separating cities from school districts. House prices jump discontinuously even when comparing homes on the same residential street, with the largest jumps where city and school district boundaries coincide. A variance decomposition attributes 13% of house price variation to local governments and 20% to neighborhoods. An equilibrium sorting model reveals substantial heterogeneity in valuations across racial groups.

JEL classification: H41, H73, R23, I22.
Keywords: Local governments, public goods, peer valuation, boundary RD.

1 Introduction

Local governments provide various essential services to households, such as education, public safety, and infrastructure. A rich history of research in economics attempts to estimate the value of these local public goods, going back to studies by Oates (1969), Brueckner (1979) and Epple and Sieg (1999), and for schools by Card and Krueger (1992), Bogart and Cromwell (1997), and Black (1999). How much households value these public goods has important implications for household sorting and the efficiency of public goods provision (Samuelson, 1954; Tiebout, 1956; Bewley, 1981; Hoxby, 1999), but compelling evidence on the valuation of publicly provided goods and the local governments that provide them remains elusive.

Estimating these valuations is challenging for at least two reasons. First, it is hard to distinguish between households valuing access to local governments and valuation of desirable neighborhoods. Household sorting plays an important role even on a relatively small geographic scale, which has put into question whether comparing house prices across local government boundaries can serve as a reliable way to estimate the valuation of local public goods. Indeed, these boundaries often exhibit discontinuities in both prices as well as characteristics of nearby residents (Bayer et al., 2007; Boustan, 2013; Gibbons et al., 2013). Second, even if one can isolate local government valuation from neighborhood quality, most existing approaches cannot disentangle what households are paying for when they cross a local government boundary. In the United States, two distinct layers of local government—cities and school districts—provide largely non-overlapping bundles of services, yet their boundaries frequently coincide, making it difficult to attribute house price discontinuities to one level of government or the other. Existing boundary studies typically examine one layer at a time, often within a single metropolitan area (Black, 1999; Bayer et al., 2007; Boustan, 2013), so they cannot compare the two. Separately identifying the value of access to cities and school districts is crucial for understanding how public policy affects household sorting and for informing models in urban economics and local public finance.

This paper makes progress on these challenges by combining a large dataset of millions of individual property sales records with new methods to estimate preferences for thousands of local governments. We begin by implementing what Bayer et al. (2007, page 608) call the “idealized use of a boundary discontinuity design”: to “compare the prices of houses on opposite sides of a neighborhood street” that serves as a boundary. In this way, “any discontinuity in house prices would be almost completely attributable to differences in the valuation” of the local governments on either side. This across-the-street comparison is the most restrictive implementation of the boundary discontinuity design, and it allows us to credibly insulate estimates of local government valuation from the influence of neighborhoods.

To separately identify the value of cities and school districts, we exploit the fact that city and school district boundaries frequently do not coincide. In our boundary sample, we distinguish three types of boundaries: “city-only” boundaries, where the city changes but the school district is the same on both sides; “district-only” boundaries, where the school district changes but the city is the same; and “city-district” boundaries, where both layers of government change simultaneously. Comparing discontinuities across these three boundary types recovers the price of access to cities, to school districts, and to the combination of the two. We then demonstrate that these discontinuities can be used to decompose house prices into unobserved local government and neighborhood valuations in connected networks of adjacent local governments. Finally, to interpret price and racial sorting discontinuities jointly and to recover group-specific preferences, we develop a simple equilibrium sorting model and estimate its structural parameters.

These analyses yield several new insights into the valuation of local governments. First, we show that prices jump discontinuously at local government boundaries even when comparing houses on opposite sides of the same residential street. Ordering the two sides of each boundary into lower and higher-average-price sides using a hold-out sample, the price discontinuity between these two sides is about \$15,200, of which \$2,200 cannot be explained by housing or neighborhood characteristics. This suggests that about 15% of the price difference between neighboring jurisdictions reflects the valuation of the local governments themselves.

Second, these discontinuities are especially large at boundaries where cities and school districts are aligned: the jump at city-district boundaries is more than twice as large as the sum of those at city-only and district-only boundaries. This points to a strong complementarity between municipal services—such as zoning and policing—and high-quality schools, and it is the first piece of direct evidence that the two overlapping layers of local government are separately valued by households. At district-only boundaries, the price discontinuity together with the difference in school spending implies that households value a marginal dollar of school spending at about 67 cents, although the estimate is too imprecise to rule out efficient provision.

Third, we disaggregate the price discontinuities of thousands of connected boundary neighborhoods and decompose them into a neighborhood component, a local government component, and their covariance. To this end, we adapt methods from labor economics typically used to decompose variation in wages into firm and worker components (Abowd et al., 1999; Kline et al., 2020), commonly referred to as AKM, but using data on houses across space instead of workers over time. Fixed effects for neighboring houses on either side of a boundary identify neighborhood effects, and fixed effects for local governments connected by neighborhoods identify local government effects. We find that local governments explain about 13% of the variation in house prices in the connected set, and neighborhoods about 20%, with the two components slightly negatively correlated. The contribution of local governments is driven almost entirely by boundaries where city and school district lines coincide, again highlighting that the two layers of local government are separately valued and complementary in equilibrium.

Finally, to recover preferences that are heterogeneous across groups, we develop an equilibrium sorting model of households choosing residential locations near a boundary. Alongside the price discontinuities, we observe modest discontinuities in racial composition: the share of Asian buyers is about 1 percentage point (13%) higher on the higher-priced side of boundaries and the share of Hispanic buyers about 0.9 percentage points (6%) lower, although both estimates are imprecise, while the shares of White and Black buyers do not jump. The model shows that group-specific valuation of one local government over another is identified as a weighted sum of the price discontinuity and a group-specific sorting discontinuity, with weights determined by the price elasticity of residential choice. We use this relationship to estimate group-specific preferences by maximum likelihood separately at each of the 1,744 boundaries for which we observe a sufficient sample of multiple groups, and we document substantial heterogeneity in preferences across racial groups.

It is worth highlighting three limitations. First, the approach of this paper is suitable to estimate the value of the excludable portion of local public goods. But many public services provided by local governments may be non-excludable. For example, crime and pollution spill across boundaries, and local government policies to combat these problems may be of considerable value to households. In this sense, estimates provided here are a lower bound of the total valuation of local governments. However, the excludable portion of local public goods, including access to high-quality schools and police departments, is important in itself, and providing estimates of their value can be useful for theory and policy. Second, households with a high willingness to pay for a local government, such as families with school-age children, sort onto the relevant side of the boundary, and many dimensions of this sorting are unobserved. Price discontinuities may therefore overstate the average household’s valuation. The conceptual framework in Section 2 allows preferences to differ across observed groups, but not within them. Third, peer composition is itself an outcome of sorting. Peer characteristics are therefore post-treatment controls, and when households value their peers, there can be multiple equilibria: for instance, if Asian households want to live near other Asian households, they could do so in many places. We therefore report how local government valuations correlate with peer characteristics but do not identify a causal effect of peers.

Links to existing literature. This paper builds on a long literature studying how local public goods and property taxes capitalize in house prices (Oates, 1969; Brueckner, 1979, 1982; Deller, 1990; Berglas, 1984), often using boundary discontinuities (Black, 1999; Bayer et al., 2007; Boustan, 2013; Gibbons et al., 2013; Turner et al., 2014). The contribution to this literature is threefold. First, due to the large national sample of house sales available in this paper, it is the first to use the across-the-street comparison at scale to study how differences in public goods are capitalized into housing prices, holding other components of neighborhood quality fixed. This gives a bird’s-eye view of how households value local public services and schools across the United States. Second, the paper separately identifies the value of access to cities and to school districts—the two primary layers of local government in the United States—by exploiting the fact that city and school district boundaries often do not coincide. This allows us to document that the two layers are strongly complementary in equilibrium: more than twice as much of a discontinuous jump in prices occurs at boundaries where both cities and school districts change than at boundaries where only one of them changes. Third, the sorting model developed here identifies preferences for local governments even when households sort discontinuously at the boundary, extending the standard boundary discontinuity design to recover group-specific valuations. Heterogeneous preferences are not new in this setting: Bayer et al. (2007) embed a boundary discontinuity design in a residential choice model with heterogeneous preferences for school and neighborhood attributes within one metropolitan area. What is new here is that the model recovers each group’s valuation of one local government over another from the price and sorting discontinuities at that boundary, estimated separately at each of 1,744 boundaries across 142 metropolitan areas.

A related quasi-experimental literature estimates the returns to local public spending, including school facility investments (Cellini et al., 2010; Martorell et al., 2016; Baron, 2022; Lafortune and Schönholzer, 2022; Biasi et al., 2025), and tests whether spending is efficient by comparing its capitalization with its cost (Barrow and Rouse, 2004; Cellini et al., 2010; Bayer et al., 2026). We apply this comparison to both cities and school districts across the country, although boundaries do not provide the exogenous variation in spending and taxes that these studies exploit.

A number of studies propose methods to capture unobserved neighborhood quality, amenities, and household sorting for valuation of local public goods (Gyourko and Tracy, 1991; Kahn, 1995; Bogart and Cromwell, 1997; Bayer et al., 2007; Caetano, 2019; Albouy et al., 2020; Laliberté, 2021). Bogart and Cromwell (1997) are the first to exploit the fact that city and school district boundaries do not always overlap to estimate school valuation separately from city valuation. The approach presented here is most similar to Laliberté (2021), who uses boundary discontinuities in student achievement across school attendance zones in Montreal combined with a student-mover design to separate neighborhood effects from school effects. In contrast, we show that connected sets of boundary discontinuities can be used directly to decompose neighborhood and local government valuation and to study how they correlate with observable characteristics. This methodology has wide applicability across many settings in local public finance, urban economics, development, and history.

Finally, this paper relates to a literature on household valuation of peers and sorting in theories of local public goods provision (Brueckner and Lee, 1989; de Bartolome, 1990; Benabou, 1993; Durlauf, 1996; Epple and Romano, 1998; Sethi and Somanathan, 2004; Calabrese et al., 2006; Bayer et al., 2022). The correlations of local government valuations with peer characteristics that we report are consistent with the view that peer composition is an important input into household residential choice (Rothstein, 2006; Abdulkadiroğlu et al., 2020), though the correlational nature of our projections means we do not attempt to identify a causal role for peers separately from the bundled public services they co-consume.

Section 4 presents boundary discontinuity estimates using the across-the-street design, followed by Section 5 describing the house price decomposition into a local government and neighborhood component. 2 Conceptual Framework

We develop a simple conceptual framework to make the distinction between local governments and neighborhoods precise, drawing on ideas from Bilal and Rossi-Hansberg (2021). On the supply side, we consider a residential neighborhood that straddles a local government boundary with smoothly varying neighborhood quality but a different bundle of public goods (and peers with whom to share them) on either side of the boundary. On the demand side, we are interested in identifying the valuation of both neighborhoods and local governments. Hence, we allow for households to sort endogenously both within and across each of the local governments on either side of the boundary. In equilibrium, patterns of household sorting and prices are pinned down by the combination of locational amenities (neighborhood and local government valuation) and household preferences. The main lesson is that a group’s valuation of one local government over another equals the price jump at the boundary plus the jump in the group’s presence there, converted into dollars by how sensitive the group’s location choices are to prices. We use this framework to interpret the boundary discontinuities in Section 4 and estimate it boundary by boundary in Section 6.

Agents. Households are divided into groups \(g\in \mathcal{G} = \{1,...,G\}\), which in our application are racial and ethnic groups. Each group makes up a share \(m_g\) of the households who buy in the neighborhood. Housing supply is fixed: each location has a unit of uniform housing supply.1 The local governments’ services and taxes on either side of the boundary are taken as given.

Choice set. Each household chooses a single location \(s \in \mathcal{S} = [-0.5, 0.5]\) in a neighborhood that straddles a boundary at \(s = 0\) separating two local governments. The households who buy in the neighborhood are taken as given: the framework describes where they locate within the neighborhood, not whether they buy there, so there is no outside option.

Market. The market of interest is a single boundary neighborhood. Housing at each location \(s\) in the neighborhood trades at a price \(p(s)\) that is the same for all groups, and the market clears location by location. Each neighborhood is a separate market: households do not choose between neighborhoods, so prices in one neighborhood do not respond to conditions in another.2 Without an outside option, adding a constant to \(p(s)\) everywhere leaves every choice unchanged, so the framework pins down prices only up to a constant. We therefore only use price differences within a neighborhood, in particular the discontinuity at the boundary.

Objective. A household in group \(g\) chooses \(s\) to maximize \(U_g(s) = V_g(s) + \sigma_g \varepsilon_g(s)\). The systematic component of utility is given by

\[ V_{g}(s)=\mu_{g}(s)+\psi_{g}(s)-p(s), \]

where \(\mu_g(s)\) captures group-specific average valuation of amenities that varies smoothly across the neighborhood (including across the local government boundary); \(\psi_g(s)\) is group-specific average valuation of local governments, which is constant within local governments but may jump at the boundary; and \(p(s)\) is the equilibrium price for housing at \(s\), which applies to households irrespective of their group membership. Households may value a local government \(\psi_g(s)\) directly because of the local public goods it provides, or indirectly because of strategic complementarities of sharing the local government with a particular set of households residing there in equilibrium. The stochastic component of utility, \(\sigma_g \varepsilon_g(s)\), consists of an identically and independently distributed type-I extreme value random variable \(\varepsilon_g(s)\) and a dispersion parameter \(\sigma_g\) that captures the relative weight of idiosyncratic drivers of residential choice not captured in neighborhood amenities, local governments, or prices.3

Optimal choice. Under utility maximization, the density of households of group \(g\) across the neighborhood, denoted by \(f_g(s)\), is

\[ f_{g}(s)=\frac{\exp\left(V_{g}(s)/\sigma_{g}\right)}{\int_{\mathcal{S}}\exp\left(V_{g}(\ell)/\sigma_{g}\right)d\ell}, \tag{1} \]

as shown in Appendix F.

Equilibrium. An allocation of households across the neighborhood and a price for each location are a sorting equilibrium if households choose locations according to (1) and the housing market clears in the neighborhood, which requires \(\sum_{g\in \mathcal{G}}m_{g}f_{g}(s)=1\) at every location. The left-hand side is the demand for housing at a location across all groups, taking into account the size of each group, whereas the right-hand side is the unit supply of housing at each location. Intuitively, if demand exceeds supply at some location, then the price at this location has to rise to dissuade some households from choosing to live there.

Figure 1 illustrates the relationship between preferences, prices, and residential choice in a sorting equilibrium. Three groups have preferences for amenities and local governments as shown in Panel A. In particular, households in Group 1 and 2 have a preference for the local government on the right of the boundary, whereas those in Group 3 have a preference for the one on the left. Panel B shows the corresponding equilibrium price schedule, which exhibits a discontinuity at the boundary. Finally, Panel C shows the equilibrium sorting behavior of the three groups. In line with their preferences, households in Group 2 and 3 sort discontinuously on the side of the boundary of their preferred local government, but interestingly, those in Group 1 sort on the left side even though they prefer the government on the right. The reason for this is that households in Group 2 have such a strong preference for the right government that many of those in Group 1 are not willing to pay the resulting market premium to live there.4

Identification. The illustration in Figure 1 shows how preferences yield equilibrium prices and sorting behavior. But the econometrician solves the reverse problem: using observed prices and sorting behavior to infer preferences. To see how preferences are identified from observed prices and sorting patterns, define for any variable \(x(s)\) its boundary discontinuity \(\Delta x \equiv x(0) - \lim_{s\rightarrow 0^-} x(s)\). Then, we can express a group’s preference for the government on the right of the boundary over the one on the left as

\[ \Delta \psi_g = \sigma_g \Delta \log f_g + \Delta p. \tag{2} \]

We can see that these preferences are identified as the weighted sum of the price discontinuity \(\Delta p\), which applies to all groups in the same way, and the group-specific sorting discontinuity \(\Delta \log f_g\), which captures a group’s willingness to pay a premium above and beyond the market price.5 The weight on the sorting discontinuity is given by the dispersion parameter \(\sigma_g\): if a group’s preferences are more idiosyncratic (i.e. larger \(\sigma_g\)), then systematic sorting implies a larger willingness to pay to live in one government over another. In contrast to local government preferences, the dispersion parameter \(\sigma_g\) is identified by variation in residential choices and prices away from the boundary.6

Multiple equilibria. The structure of this framework is very similar to Bayer et al. (2004), and the argument that an equilibrium exists is essentially identical.7 Without strategic complementarities, the equilibrium is also unique, with prices unique up to a constant (Bayer et al., 2004, Proposition 1). When households value the peers they share a local government with, however, there can be multiple equilibria: for instance, if Asian households want to live near other Asian households, they could concentrate on either side of the boundary. Multiplicity does not affect identification. Equation (2) follows from households’ optimal choices in (1) alone, so it holds in whichever equilibrium is realized at a boundary. The estimated \(\psi_g\) is therefore a group’s valuation of the local government as it is in that equilibrium, including any value the group places on the households who live there. For this reason we do not use the estimates to predict how households would re-sort if a local government changed, which would require choosing among equilibria, and we report how \(\psi_g\) correlates with peer characteristics rather than attempting to estimate causal effects of peers.

Example: Piedmont-Oakland. It is useful to see Equation (2) in action, using an example from our data. Piedmont, CA is an affluent city and school district in the San Francisco Bay Area whose residents are mostly White and Asian, whereas neighboring Oakland is much larger and more diverse, both in terms of household income and race. Figure 2 shows the price and sorting discontinuities near the boundary between the two local governments. The panel on the left shows that prices for similar housing jump by about \$172,000 at the boundary, corresponding to the \(\Delta p\) term in Equation (2). The right panel shows that White households sort discontinuously on the less expensive Oakland side, whereas Asian households are more likely to reside on the more expensive Piedmont side. Transforming these discontinuities in White and Asian shares at the boundary into densities and taking the difference of their logs yields sorting discontinuity estimates of \(-0.146\) for \(\Delta\log f_{\text{White}}\) and \(0.710\) for \(\Delta\log f_{\text{Asian}}\), respectively. Finally, price variation relative to variation in the (log) densities of the two groups yields estimates of \$123,000 for \(\sigma_{\text{White}}\) and \$103,000 for \(\sigma_{\text{Asian}}\).8

Putting these numbers together according to Equation (2), we get preference estimates of White households for Piedmont over Oakland of \$154,000 and for Asian households of \$245,000. Even though White households prefer Piedmont just like Asian households, the stronger preference of Asian households and the resulting higher price to live there dissuades some White households from residing in Piedmont, leading to a discontinuous drop of White households when crossing from Oakland into Piedmont.

3 Setting and Data

3.1 Local Governments in the U.S.

The bulk of local public goods in the U.S. in terms of expenditures are provided by school districts and municipalities (henceforth “cities”). Unlike in most countries in the world, the vast majority of school districts and cities are governed separately in the United States, with some school districts providing services to multiple cities, such as the Los Angeles Unified district to 26 cities in the region; and sometimes a city is serviced by multiple school districts, such as Phoenix or San Jose, each having multiple elementary school districts offering education services.

The top panel of Appendix Figure A.1 shows the spatial distribution of all 19,539 cities and 13,193 school districts in the United States. As we can see, the distribution differs markedly by region: In New England, there are often more school districts than cities, while in the South there are relatively more cities and generally a sparser distribution of local governments. Major metropolitan areas are often densely packed with dozens of local governments clustered around a central city.

Local governments provide a wide variety of services (see Appendix Figure A.2). As shown in Appendix Table A.1, both cities and school districts vary enormously in size, demographic characteristics, and per capita expenditures. School districts are primarily responsible for K-12 education provision to any household in their jurisdiction. Cities have a broad portfolio of responsibilities. Their largest expenditure item and their most important responsibility is public safety, including police and fire protection. On average, cities spend about \$300 per capita on police protection and \$100 per capita on fire protection. Local services include a number of other items too, such as street maintenance, utilities, parks and recreation, libraries, sewerage maintenance, and solid waste management.

Many of the public goods provided by school districts and cities are excludable to non-residents. While school districts sometimes have programs for non-residents to join (Bergman, 2018), residency in the district is usually required to attend a school in the district (Education Law Center, 2005). Similarly, access to services such as police, fire protection, and emergency medical services are tightly linked to residence as well: calls to 9-1-1 are routed to the jurisdiction associated with the address. Zoning, regulation, and other local ordinances are also sharply tied to residence, although they may exert local externalities in the neighborhood (Turner et al., 2014).

If a household highly values any of these excludable services, it is thus usually required to reside in the associated jurisdiction. The extent to which excludable public goods are important is then reflected in a sharp difference in capitalization of local governments at the boundary. In contrast, other local public goods and neighborhood amenities are not excludable. As a result, a household can still enjoy these amenities even when living nearby but outside of the jurisdiction, and they would be capitalized smoothly across local government boundaries.

3.2 Data

To separately identify valuations of local governments and their neighborhoods, we combine two primary datasets. First, we use fine-grained local government boundaries from the Census TIGER/Line database (U.S. Census Bureau, 2011). Second, we combine these boundaries with a large sample of individual property records from ATTOM (ATTOM Data Solutions, 2021), which report the price of each house’s most recent sale.9 We supplement these data with standard records from the American Community Survey (ACS), from the Stanford Education Data Archive (SEDA), and Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR). We describe each of the primary datasets in turn.

Local government boundaries. Local governance in the U.S. consists of various entities including counties, school districts, incorporated towns and cities, townships, and special districts. We focus on school districts as well as incorporated towns and cities (jointly referred to as cities) as the two most important layers of local governance as they make up the bulk of local public employment, revenue and expenditures.

To measure the extent of these two types of jurisdictions, we use shapefiles from the Census’s TIGER/Line place boundaries database. These consist of detailed boundaries for each place. We keep the more than 19,000 incorporated places and drop Census Designated Places (CDPs), which are statistical units for the census and provide no services of their own. Areas outside any city are unincorporated, and there we treat the county as the local government that provides city services. School district boundaries have been prepared by the NCES’s EDGE school district boundaries program (National Center for Education Statistics, 2014), which also draws on the TIGER/Line database. It combines elementary school districts and unified school district boundaries that jointly cover all of the U.S. with more than 13,000 districts.10

City-district pairs. We use these boundaries to create city-district pairs, which we simply call local governments. The territorial extent of such a local government is a subset of an overlapping city and district territory, and in case city and district boundaries do not coincide, the local government is a strict subset. This means that neighboring local governments are often in the same city but different school districts or vice versa. This is illustrated in Appendix Figure A.3: the blue boundary in the Northwest separates the City of Cupertino in the North from the City of Saratoga in the South. The green boundary in the South divides the Cupertino Unified School District (CUSD) in the North from the Saratoga Unified School District (SUSD) in the South. Hence local government ① consists of the Cupertino city-district pair; local government ② of the City of Saratoga but the Cupertino school district; and local government ③ combines the Saratoga city-district pair.

ATTOM property records. ATTOM offers a comprehensive database of individual property records drawing primarily on county assessor records, which also report each property’s most recent sale, and covering a total of 118 million properties over 2003-2016, with near-universal coverage of properties for a little more than half of all states. These records have been standardized and cleaned across jurisdictions, allowing us to focus on comparable properties across the country. We restrict our sample to single family residential properties since multi-unit residential properties and commercial properties are harder to compare. For each property, we have information on the sales price at the time of the most recent transaction, the most recent property tax payment, its geocoded location, age, lot size, indoor square footage, the number of rooms, the number of bathrooms, and the number of stories. Since houses were last sold in different years, we express prices in 2010 dollars using the CPI-U (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2021) and control for year-of-sale fixed effects throughout. The average year of sale is the same on both sides of boundaries (Table 1). We use last names of homebuyers to probabilistically infer their race and ethnicity using the Bayesian procedure in Diamond et al. (2019, see Appendix B.2 for details). To this end, we merge property records with census data (U.S. Census Bureau, 2016) assigning probabilities to last names according to race and ethnicity into five categories: White, Black, Asian, Hispanic, and Unknown. The latter category applies when no posterior probability of any race reaches 80%.11

3.3 Boundary sample and Neighborhoods

Next, we link these 25 million houses to the nearest local government boundary, restricting the matching to properties within 500 meters (0.31 miles) of any boundary. We include only houses that are near boundaries with a sufficient number of houses on either side of the boundary. Specifically, we require at least 100 houses on each side of the boundary, with further restrictions on sufficient density of houses as detailed in Appendix B.3. We define neighborhoods as 500-meter segments along these boundaries (the white diamonds in Appendix Figure A.3) and associate each house with the nearest one of these neighborhoods. Neighborhoods with no properties in the immediate vicinity (100m), which may be due to a physical barrier running along the boundary, are dropped together with their associated houses. To ensure neighborhood continuity across boundaries, we drop boundary segments that cross a highway or other major roadway and keep only those that coincide with residential streets, as can be seen in the center of Appendix Figure A.3. This means that neighborhoods running across major roadways such as 8 Mile Road in Detroit are excluded. This definition of neighborhoods guarantees that there is a substantial number of neighborhoods that cut across local government boundaries. If we were to use census blocks or tracts instead, we would have very little variation in local governments within census units.

This results in a boundary sample of 1.47 million properties, which are slightly more expensive and less likely to have been bought by a buyer with a White surname. Since there are many more houses near boundaries in dense urban areas, houses have on average substantially smaller lot sizes and are slightly older, although they have a higher average number of rooms. They are in neighborhoods that are less White, substantially more Hispanic and Asian, and have slightly higher household income at around \$71,100.

The boundary sample covers just over a thousand cities and school districts, as shown in columns 4-6 in Table A.1. These cities and school districts are substantially larger than the average city and school district across the country. They are also more diverse, have higher average household income, higher share of college-educated residents, and higher revenue and expenditures, as one would expect from local governments in high-density urban areas.

4 Discontinuities at Local Government Boundaries

We now estimate discontinuities in prices, the racial composition of buyers, and property taxes across local government boundaries. As in the framework of Section 2, we assume that amenities vary smoothly across the boundary, so a jump in prices or in the composition of buyers at the boundary reflects differences between the local governments rather than between neighborhoods. We narrow the comparison step by step, down to similar houses on the same street but on opposite sides of the boundary.

Empirical approach. We begin by stacking all boundaries in our data after orienting them such that the side with the lower average price is on the left and the side with the higher average price on the right. To avoid orienting boundaries on the outcome itself, we determine the orientation only from houses 400 to 500 meters from the boundary and estimate discontinuities on the houses within 400 meters. Concretely, we run

\[ p_{in} = \alpha_n + \psi D_{in}+g_n(S_{in})+X_{in}'\gamma+\epsilon_{in}, \tag{3} \]

where houses \(i=1,...,I_n\) are nested in \(n = 1,...,N\) neighborhoods. \(S_{in}\) is the distance to the nearest boundary and \(p_{in}\) is the price paid in the most recent sale of house \(i\). \(D_{in} = \mathbf{1}[S_{in} \geq 0]\) indicates the high-price side, so \(\psi\) is the average price discontinuity. \(\alpha_n\) are neighborhood fixed effects, and \(g_n(\cdot)\) is a smooth function of distance that captures gradual changes in neighborhood quality toward the boundary. We add controls \(X_{in}\) step by step: cluster-by-year-of-sale fixed effects throughout, then the race of the buyer, then housing characteristics (age, lot size, interior square feet, and fixed effects for the number of rooms, bathrooms, and stories), and finally street fixed effects. With neighborhood fixed effects alone, equation (3) is a standard boundary discontinuity design, estimated on boundary segments that coincide with residential streets (Section 3). Adding street fixed effects restricts the comparison to houses on the same street but on opposite sides of the boundary. This across-the-street comparison is the most restrictive implementation of the boundary discontinuity design possible.

After residualizing prices on the fixed effects and controls, we estimate equation (3) in two ways: nonparametrically, by local linear regression with the MSE-optimal bandwidth of Calonico et al. (2014) and a uniform kernel (“CCT”); and parametrically, with separate quartic polynomials on either side of the boundary within 400 meters (“Quartic”). Standard errors are clustered by boundary.

Price discontinuities. Table 1 presents a simple comparison of houses on the low and high average-price side of local government boundaries. In the hold-out band 400 to 500 meters from the boundary, houses on the high side sell for \$284,900 on average, \$31,700 more than on the low side, and within 100 meters of the boundary the difference is still \$20,600. Houses on the high side are slightly newer and larger, their buyers are slightly more likely to be White or Asian, and their neighborhoods have more college-educated and high-income households. The question is how much of this difference is due to the local governments rather than to gradually changing neighborhoods.

Figure 3 shows the across-the-street estimates of equation (3), with all controls, using 2% sample bins and local linear regressions with a bandwidth of 160 meters, close to the average CCT bandwidth of 140 meters. Pooling all boundaries (top left), prices on the low side are flat at around \$267,000 up to the boundary and jump to around \$269,000 on the high side. There is little sign that prices rise gradually toward the high side, as they would if the price difference merely reflected improving neighborhood quality.

Estimated separately by boundary type, the discontinuity is about \$2,200 at district-only boundaries (bottom left; \$1,400 and not statistically significant with the quartic), small and not statistically significant at city-only boundaries (top right), and \$6,200 to \$7,200, or 2% to 3% of house prices, at boundaries where both the city and the district change (bottom right; Table 2, row 5). City boundaries that do not coincide with a district boundary thus add little value on their own, while joint city-district boundaries show a larger jump than the city-only and district-only boundaries combined. Appendix D discusses why cities may be valued mainly together with aligned school districts.

Implied efficiency of local spending. Local governments on the high-price side also differ in property taxes, spending, and the households they serve. Specifically, they serve smaller populations of richer households, and their school districts spend more per pupil and have higher student achievement (Appendix E, Appendix Figure A.9 and Appendix Table A.3). Comparing these differences with the price discontinuity provides a test of whether local public goods are efficiently provided: if a jurisdiction spends efficiently, a marginal dollar of spending financed by a dollar of taxes should have no effect on house prices (Brueckner, 1982; Cellini et al., 2010; Bayer et al., 2026). In our setting, the comparison of boundaries where only the school district changes provides the most precise estimate of efficiency. The comparison of the price and property tax discontinuities of these boundaries implies that households value the higher school spending on the high-price side at about 67 cents per dollar of spending, with a 95% confidence interval from −\$0.16 to \$1.49 (Appendix E and Appendix Table A.14 show the calculation and its standard errors). A marginal dollar of school spending is thus valued at less than its cost, although the confidence interval does not rule out efficient provision. Because boundaries do not provide exogenous variation in spending and taxes, this ratio is a descriptive comparison rather than a causal estimate of the value of a marginal dollar of spending.

5 Comparing Local Government and Neighborhood Valuation

Empirical approach. Stacking boundaries ordered by the average price in the 400-500m bins from the boundary allows us to detect a discontinuity, provided that the discontinuity is in the direction of the average price change. But this assumption may not be satisfied, and ordering boundaries in this way removes a lot of interesting heterogeneity across local governments. In particular, we are interested in how local government valuation compares to the valuation of neighborhoods, local public goods, and peers who co-reside in the local government.

We can avoid ordering boundaries by exploiting the fact that the sample of neighborhoods form a bipartite network of local governments in space connected by mutual boundaries, as shown in Appendix Figure A.10. It turns out that this data structure can be organized along the same lines as two-way fixed effects models estimating unobserved worker and firm fixed effects in labor economics (Abowd et al., 1999, henceforth AKM), whereby we use neighborhoods “moving” across local governments through space instead of workers moving across firms over time. As we demonstrate in Appendix C, the conditions for separate identification of unobserved neighborhood and local government effects are identical to AKM.

Clusters of connected local governments. Local government effects can only be compared among local governments that are linked by a chain of boundary neighborhoods, just as firm effects in AKM can only be compared among firms linked by workers moving between them. Figure 5 illustrates this with three local governments \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\): the price jump in neighborhood 1 identifies \(\psi_B - \psi_A\), the jump in neighborhood 2 identifies \(\psi_B - \psi_C\), and together they identify \(\psi_C - \psi_A\), even though \(A\) and \(C\) share no boundary neighborhood. As each local government may border several other local governments, they form metropolitan clusters in the form of a spatial network of connected local governments. Within a cluster, each local government effect is identified relative to one reference local government. Since, by definition, neighborhoods do not link separate clusters, effects in different clusters cannot be compared without further assumptions, which we discuss in Section 5.2. These clusters arise entirely endogenously based on the availability of ATTOM property records across states as well as sufficient density of houses near boundaries. As shown in the bottom panel of Appendix Figure A.1, they are similar to standard definitions of metropolitan areas such as Consolidated Metropolitan Statistical Areas (CMSAs) as defined by the census, but diverge in important ways. First, large CMSAs often have multiple clusters within them that are not connected. For example, there are two large clusters with more than 20 connected local governments each on Long Island. In total, there are 444 clusters with an average number of 4.43 local governments. The largest cluster is Los Angeles with 73 connected local governments.

As an example of a cluster, see Appendix Figure A.10, the cluster around San Jose, CA (and the second-largest in the country). We can see that most of Silicon Valley is connected through residential neighborhoods as demonstrated in the Cupertino-Saratoga example in Appendix Figure A.3, which can be found in the Southwest on the map in Appendix Figure A.10. The red boundaries show connected local governments that are separated by stacked city-district boundaries, such as the boundary between Palo Alto and Los Altos in the West. Blue boundaries separate cities, with either side of the boundary belonging to the same school district; and green boundaries separate districts, with either side receiving services from the same city.

We can implement this strategy empirically through the regression

\[ p_{in}=\alpha_{n}+\psi_{j(i,n)}+g\left(S_{in}\right)+X_{in}'\gamma+\epsilon_{in} \tag{4} \]

where \(p_{in}\) is now the log price of house \(i\), \(\alpha_n\) is the average valuation of neighborhood \(n\), and \(\psi_{j(i,n)}\) is the average valuation for local government \(j = j(i,n)\) associated with house \(i\) in neighborhood \(n\), depending on which side of the boundary house \(i\) is located. \(X_{in}\) are the same housing characteristics as in equation (3) as well as state-by-year fixed effects. \(g(S_{in})\) are separate quartic polynomials on either side of the boundary. Together, \(\alpha_n\) and \(g(S_{in})\) approximate the smoothly varying neighborhood quality \(\mu(s)\) in Figure 5. Identifying assumptions are discussed in detail in Appendix C. This decomposition allows neighborhood and local government valuations to be correlated, but it assumes they are additively separable, which we can test by comparing this model to a saturated model (see Section 5.2).

We use log prices here, unlike in Section 4, so that variance shares are comparable across clusters with very different price levels: in levels, the most expensive clusters would dominate the pooled variance. We then decompose the variance in log house prices into

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(p_{in}) & =\text{Var}(\alpha_{n})+\text{Var}(\psi_{j(i,n)})+\text{Var}(X_{in}'\gamma)+\text{Var}(\epsilon_{in})\\ & \quad+2\text{Cov}(\alpha_{n},\psi_{j(i,n)})+2 \text{Cov}(\psi_{j(i,n)},X_{in}'\gamma)+2 \text{Cov}(\alpha_{n},X_{in}'\gamma) \end{aligned} \tag{5} \]

where we are particularly interested in the contributions of the neighborhood component \(\text{Var}\left(\alpha_{n}\right)\) and the local government component \(\text{Var}(\psi_{j(i,n)})\). These components tell us how much of the variation in house prices is explained through differences in neighborhood and local government valuation. In addition, \(\text{Cov}(\alpha_{n},\psi_{j(i,n)})\) indicates the extent to which homebuyers seem to treat neighborhoods and local governments as complements or substitutes, depending on whether it is positive or negative. Since plug-in estimates as in AKM or Card et al. (2013) are known to be biased, we also present estimates using the leave-one-out estimator developed by Kline et al. (2020, henceforth KSS), which estimates bias-corrected variance components with unrestricted heterogeneity.

In addition to estimating variance components using equation (5), we can also investigate how the estimated local government effects correlate with observed characteristics of corresponding cities and school districts. While these estimates are not causal, they demonstrate what aspects of local governments are valued in equilibrium. We study this by running

\[ \widehat{\psi}_{j}=\delta_{h(j)} + Z_{j}'\beta+r_{j}, \]

where \(\delta_{h(j)}\) are city or district fixed effects, \(Z_j\) is a vector of observable city and school district characteristics, and \(r_j\) is an error term. Including city or district fixed effects restricts the comparison to cities in the same school district or vice versa.

5.2 Connected Cluster Variance Decomposition

Pooling clusters requires a normalization, since local government effects are identified only up to a constant within each cluster. We first residualize log house prices on cluster fixed effects, so that between-cluster differences in average prices do not drive the variation. We then set the effect of one reference local government equal across all clusters. Specifically, we use the part of the largest city (by population) that lies in its largest school district as the reference jurisdiction, similar to using establishments in the restaurant industry as the benchmark across disconnected sets in the worker-firm setting (Card et al., 2024). Comparisons across clusters therefore assume that households value these reference local governments alike across metro areas.

We begin our analysis of pooled clusters by investigating to what extent the data supports the assumption that local government and neighborhood valuations are additive. To this end, Appendix Table A.4 compares the additive model with a saturated model with unrestricted neighborhood-by-local-government effects. The adjusted \(R^2\) rises only from 0.65 to 0.66. With 1.47 million observations, a formal F test rejects the additive restrictions, but the match component is small: after correcting for estimation noise, its variance is about 8% of the variance of the local government component and about 1% of the variance of log house prices.15

5.3 Projecting Valuation on Observables

We now study which characteristics of local governments are correlated with their valuation. These correlations are not causal, but they show which characteristics households value in equilibrium. We group the characteristics into outputs (student achievement and crime clearance rates), inputs (expenditures and public employment per capita), and peers (average household income and racial composition from the American Community Survey, measured for the city or school district whose valuation we project).16 Inputs speak to sorting on public services, peers to whom the services are shared with, and outputs to the quality of services, which may reflect both inputs and peers.

Estimated valuations are highly correlated with student achievement and essentially uncorrelated with crime clearance rates (Appendix Figure A.13). Table 6 adds inputs, peers, and city or district fixed effects.17 Without peer characteristics (column 1), a one-standard deviation increase in student achievement is associated with about 12% higher valuation, but the correlation is much weaker and not statistically significant once peer characteristics are included. Crime clearance rates are not correlated with valuation when comparing cities in the same school district, with or without peer characteristics. Peer characteristics themselves are modestly correlated with valuation in districts. For cities, peers play by far the largest role, with average household income being strongly predictive of city valuations. These estimates suggest that, on average, households shy away from local governments with lower household incomes, perhaps because they expect public services to be overwhelmed or because city zoning may reinforce patterns of residential segregation.

6 Estimating Group-Specific Preferences

The variance decompositions and correlations with observables in the previous section show that local government valuations are closely correlated with peer characteristics. But two limitations of the AKM approach stand out. First, it relies on correlations that are difficult to interpret without a model of how prices and sorting are jointly determined. Second, households may have heterogeneous preferences for local governments, and the average valuations estimated via fixed effects cannot account for such differences. Estimating the AKM model separately by the race of the buyer does not solve this problem (Appendix Table A.9). If buyers of all groups pay the same price for the same house, then the group-specific effects can differ from the pooled ones only because groups buy different houses in different local governments, not because they value the same local government differently. Accordingly, the group-specific effects are highly correlated with the pooled effects, with correlations of 0.70 to 0.92. Differences in preferences instead show up in where groups choose to live, which is the variation the framework exploits. We therefore estimate the framework from Section 2 separately at each boundary to recover group-specific preferences.

6.1 Estimation

We residualize house prices with respect to housing characteristics and year of transaction and estimate the framework separately for each of the 1,744 boundaries in our dataset, using all houses within 500 meters of the boundary, for which there are a sufficient number of households of at least two groups. We estimate local government preferences via maximum likelihood as shown in Appendix F. The price coefficient in the choice probabilities (1) is \(1/\sigma_g\), so it differs across groups. We set \(\sigma_g\) separately for each group and boundary from the ratio of the variation in prices to the variation in the log density of the group within local governments, as described in Appendix F.

6.2 Results

Panel A of Appendix Table A.5 shows the means and standard deviations for estimates of the dispersion parameters \(\sigma_g\) for each group and of the absolute value of each group’s preference for one local government over the other, \(|\Delta\psi_g|\). In line with each group’s average household income, the implied price elasticities (i.e. the inverse of \(\sigma_g\)) are low for White households, moderately high for Asian and Hispanic households, and high for Black households. Turning to the local government preference differentials, Asian and Black households show the largest willingness to pay to live in one over the other local government (around \$30,000), whereas the average absolute differential for White households is relatively small at around \$20,000. Panel B shows the correlations of estimated local government preferences across groups, suggesting that Asian and White households are most closely correlated (\(0.34\)), whereas Asian and Black households are least closely correlated (\(-0.12\)).

In Table 7, we regress estimates of group-specific local government preference differentials \(\Delta\psi_g\) on differences in the same observable local government characteristics – output, inputs, and peers – as we did for estimates of average valuation \(\psi_j\) in Table 6. That is, we run the following regressions:

\[ \widehat{\Delta\psi}_{g,n} = \Delta Z_n'\beta_g + \xi_{g,n}, \]

where \(\Delta Z_n\) are the difference of a vector of characteristics of the local government to the right and the one to the left of the boundary. Similar to Table 6, our interpretation of the resulting coefficients is that they reveal local government characteristics that are correlated with group-specific preferences in equilibrium, although these need not correspond to causal effects of these characteristics. Unlike the projections in Table 6, these boundary-level regressions do not rely on the assumptions of the AKM decomposition. They also use more information than the price discontinuity at each boundary, which is too imprecisely estimated to be projected on its own (Appendix Figure A.4).

7 Conclusion

This paper provides precise estimates of house price and racial sorting discontinuities at local government boundaries, comparing only houses on the same street. We show that there is substantial heterogeneity in the implied preferences for local governments, both across racial groups and types of governments. We briefly discuss implications for efficiency and policy as well as areas for future research.

A fundamental debate in local public finance since its inception as a field is the question whether public goods can be efficiently provided. Samuelson (1954) worried that the externalities embedded in public goods would lead to freeriding and underprovision, whereas Tiebout (1956) suggested that household sorting across local governments would restore efficiency. At district-only boundaries, we find that households value a marginal dollar of school spending at about 67 cents, though confidence intervals are too wide to exclude efficient provision (Brueckner, 1982). Furthermore, if peer characteristics play an important role in residential choice, then efficient Tiebout sorting is unlikely. Brueckner and Lee (1989) formalize this tension: when one group benefits from the presence of another, mixed communities can be more efficient than homogeneous Tiebout sorting, albeit at the cost of an efficiency loss from having to tailor public goods to average rather than individual tastes. The correlation of local government valuations with peer characteristics suggests that this tension is relevant in practice, although we do not identify a causal effect of peers.

The findings in this paper also highlight the potential benefits and tradeoffs of designing place-based policy to target local governments as opposed to neighborhoods: neighborhoods account for a larger share of house price variation than local governments, but local governments account for a substantial share on their own, particularly where city and school district boundaries coincide.

Finally, an important area of future research suggested by the findings of this paper is the role of non-excludable local public goods. Much of what local governments do could have substantial spillovers on adjacent neighborhoods and local governments. Quantifying the share of non-excludable public goods in household valuation makes for an interesting problem for future research to tackle.
